Loki / working product and protocol brief1 October 2026 · Design for review, not a custody claim

Shielded multisig built around the member’s decision.

The difficult part is not producing a threshold signature. It is letting people approve a Zcash payment at different times, from phones they trust, while knowing exactly what their approval authorizes and what happens when a member disappears.

The design: every member has a real local wallet and one FROST share. Devices exchange encrypted proposals and approvals. For a supported payment, the wallet prepares the possible signing groups ahead of time, so each member can verify the transaction, approve once, and leave. Any completed quorum can prove and send the payment.
The constraint Zcash adds

The chain sees one signer. The group does the work elsewhere.

A transparent multisig advertises its structure on-chain. Loki instead uses threshold spend authorization for shielded Ironwood ZEC. Distributed key generation gives each member a share sᵢ of one spend-authorizing key; no single device needs to reconstruct that classical key. Rerandomized FROST combines a quorum’s shares into an ordinary RedPallas spend authorization signature under the per-spend randomized key rk = ak + αG. The signature itself does not reveal which members participated; network or coordinator metadata still can. This follows the direction of ZIP 312, which remains a draft.

That cryptographic shape creates a product problem. FROST normally needs the same signers in two rounds: first public nonce commitments, then signature shares after a coordinator has fixed the message and signer set. A phone owner who approved in the morning cannot be assumed to return at night. A proposer can also be wrong or malicious, so sending a 32-byte sighash to the phone is not a meaningful approval experience.

The signer’s phone must know the payment, not merely the hash; the protocol must absorb the waiting, not the person.

The wallet, not a signer companion

Local-first has a precise meaning here.

Each member device scans Ironwood compact blocks with the group viewing material and keeps its own encrypted note, nullifier, memo, and transaction database. It can receive, inspect history, propose a payment, review one, and—when sufficiently synced—prove and broadcast. A desktop client may be faster, but it is another member-capable client, not the source of everyone else’s balance. The chain is authoritative for confirmed funds; a phone’s local database is its independently derived view of that chain.

Group state has a different clock. Signed proposals, approval batches, refusals, and execution notices travel through two replaceable ciphertext relays. Devices reconcile them by signed record hash. A relay can delay or hide records, but cannot invent an approval. The same sealed records can move by file when Loki’s relays disappear. Offline, the member can read cached wallet state and queue a decision; the UI separately shows the age of chain sync and group-message sync. It never calls a stale balance current.

This is why local-first matters to the UX: Bob does not need Alice’s laptop running to know what his vault contains or what he was asked to approve. It does not mean a powered-off phone can answer a later FROST request, or that an unavailable network can deliver its queued share.

Privacy cost that remainsRelays see IP addresses, timing, mailbox access, and possibly push tokens. Content-free push hides payment details from the notification service, but not that the device received a wake-up.
The asynchronous signing construction

One tap requires preparing for the people who might show up.

FROST’s first-round commitments do not depend on a payment. Members can generate nonce pairs in advance, publish only their commitments, and keep the secret nonces in a protected local store. That removes the first live round from an individual payment. It does not by itself fix the second-round problem: the transaction’s randomized spend key, sighash, and signing package still depend on which signers and commitments are chosen.

Loki’s prepared mode builds an unproved transaction candidate for every eligible t-subset. Each candidate uses distinct commitments and a randomizer derived through the pinned rerandomized-FROST API. Its recipients, amounts, memos, fee, expiry, change, and real input nullifier set must match the other candidates. The copies may have different randomized keys and ciphertext. Sharing the same input notes matters: otherwise two candidates could both mine and double-pay. A signer checks the entire family before releasing any share.

For 2-of-3, the candidates are {A,B}, {A,C}, and {B,C}. If A and C approve at different times, each phone emits its shares for the candidates it belongs to. {A,C} completes; B never needs to act. A bad share from B can break B’s candidates, while the honest {A,C} candidate remains possible. A signer is never swapped into an already fixed FROST package.

Eligibility is visible, not assumed. Every member needs enough authenticated, unassigned commitments for all of their candidate slots. Concurrent proposers can race for the same commitment; the signer’s local durable assignment decides the winner and the other proposal is rebuilt. When the family cannot be prepared, Loki states upfront that the payment needs an interactive signing visit.
What one approval actually does

The phone approves a transaction it reconstructed itself.

A notification merely wakes the app; it contains no amount or recipient. The phone fetches the encrypted proposal, validates the group certificate and signed manifest, obtains every candidate PCZT, and computes the v6 sighash locally. It then derives the screen from checked transaction fields. Contact names and the proposer’s note are marked as annotations. A first-time recipient warning comes from that device’s own history.

The phone verifies every real input belongs to the vault, all candidates use the same nullifiers, each output commitment and encrypted note agrees with the disclosed recipient/value/memo, change returns to the vault, the entire value balance implies the displayed fee, and the signed expiry matches the shown deadline. For each FROST package it checks the participant set, authenticated commitments, its own unused commitment, the recomputed randomizer, and the package message against that candidate’s locally computed sighash. Missing or redacted information is a refusal. A malformed proposal never reaches the signature code.

After the person reviews and authorizes, the device takes the assigned nonce secrets into memory, marks them spent and deletes the persisted copies before calculating shares. It saves the exact encrypted share batch before upload. If delivery fails, it resends those bytes; it never recomputes shares using a restored nonce. If it crashes after consumption but before saving the batch, those candidate slots are lost and a fresh attempt may be necessary. “Approved locally,” “delivered,” “authorized,” “broadcast,” and “confirmed” are separate states.

What v6 changesZIP 229 excludes shielded anchors from the v6 sighash. A finisher can install a current anchor and witness and prove after signatures are collected. The signature still binds the payment effects and expiry. Proving time is part of broadcast latency.

A signed cancellation message is a cooperative stop request, not revocation of shares already released. Chain expiry or a conflicting mined spend is the hard boundary; rebuilding either case needs fresh approvals.

When ordinary operations stop being ordinary

Availability is several different failures, with different answers.

If a member is offline, another prepared subset can complete. If a member sends a bad share, the finisher verifies it against the authenticated sender and identifies the faulty participant; it tries another complete candidate rather than silently rerunning the same package. If the proposer or finisher disappears, a member with the signed candidate and current chain state can prove and broadcast. If the first broadcast times out, the exact saved transaction bytes can be resent. If a relay disappears, the second relay or sealed-file exchange carries the same authenticated records. If every delivery channel is blocked, the group cannot make progress—no threshold scheme can manufacture an absent message.

Backups require a sharper distinction. Preprocessed FROST nonces are single-use secrets. A backup that restores a used nonce can expose a share if the phone signs again. Loki’s nonce store is separate from backed-up wallet history, device-bound, and consumed before signing. Each supported iOS and Android restore path must be tested. A privileged attacker who can roll back all local state on the same hardware remains outside the initial guarantee unless a proven non-rollback primitive is added. A malicious relay’s “already consumed” answer is useful telemetry, not proof of safety.

What the interface says“Waiting for Carol,” “Carol’s share failed verification,” “Ready to prove,” and “Broadcast; waiting for confirmation” are different statuses. No generic “transaction failed” screen should hide the next action.
Lost device; old share not copied

At least t surviving helpers repair the missing share. The remaining group refreshes, certifies a new device epoch, and retires old commitments. A verified set of Group Cards can provide an offline recovery route.

The group key and address may remain.
Share may be copied, or a member departs

Refresh alone cannot erase a copied threshold of old shares, and a former member retains viewing access. Create a new vault with fresh keys and make a quorum-approved shielded sweep.

A new address is the honest security boundary.
No hidden recovery promise. A single lost share can be repaired if enough other shares survive; fewer than t live shares or valid backup cards cannot reconstruct spending authority. Old t-share sets remain dangerous after refresh. All current members can view vault history, and ZIP 2005’s recovery material is not a post-quantum t-of-n guarantee.
The implementation contract

What has to be true before this holds meaningful ZEC.

The first build is a narrow regtest proof, not a mobile shell: generate compatible Ironwood viewing material and a 2-of-3 RedPallas FROST key; construct a v6 PCZT with the commitment-derived, caller-supplied α; have two independent signers verify and sign its true sighash; aggregate; install anchor and witnesses; prove; and mine the transaction. The current Orchard builder chooses α internally, so a small upstream API or maintained patch is a hard dependency. A paper reduction is also needed for many prepublished commitments and simultaneous candidate sessions.

After that, one Rust core handles wallet sync, DKG and backup, PCZT verification, FROST approval, nonce persistence, and signed transport. SwiftUI and Compose display a validated review model and call a narrow approval method; they never receive a raw sighash or nonce. The dependency versions are pinned as a tested set, and the ZIP 2005 common-root agreement and Group Card format receive independent review. Native platform work concentrates on key wrapping, restore behavior, background delivery, and accessibility.

Why the order mattersBuilding the app first would make the hardest assumption look solved by a polished screen. The caller-supplied randomizer and deferred-proof path either work end to end or change the product promise.
  1. M0
    Real cryptographic feasibility

    Mine a 2-of-3 Ironwood spend on regtest with caller-selected α and proof after authorization.

  2. M1
    A member-owned wallet

    Local mobile scan, note selection, reorg handling, receive, prove, and broadcast without a Desk.

  3. M2
    Verifiable setup and recovery material

    DKG, common viewing/recovery root, group certificate, independently readable cards, and a full restore drill.

  4. M3
    A signer that refuses correctly

    Field-by-field PCZT binding; mutated outputs, memos, fees, inputs, randomizers, and packages yield zero shares.

  5. M4
    Asynchronous approval under faults

    All allowed subsets, durable nonce journal, crash points, concurrent proposals, malicious relays, and file fallback.

  6. M5–6
    Native UX and custody release

    Real-device restore tests, comprehension tests, independent security review, measured latency and battery, then a small-value canary.

Release wording is gated. “Any t approve once” requires a complete, nonconflicted prepared family and successfully queued share batches. “No server dependency” requires a relay-shutdown spend using sealed-file exchange and independent chain endpoints. “Safe recovery” requires the actual repair, migration, and card drills. None of these follow from FROST alone.
